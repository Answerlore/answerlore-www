The design argument

Confidently wrong is the expensive kind of wrong.

In this trade a wrong answer isn't a bad email. It's a misdiagnosed compressor, a warranty claim you can't defend, a gas appliance serviced from a guess, and a second truck roll to fix what the first visit got wrong. Every AI tool can answer. The question is what it does when it shouldn't.

The experiment your industry already ran

HVAC professionals tested ChatGPT on trade questions and reached a verdict: the answers “look convincing... but often aren't actually correct.”1 A technician standing in front of a unit cannot check a confident paragraph against anything: no page number to open, no revision to compare, no way to tell a right answer from a fluent one.

And the liability is yours: a tribunal held Air Canada responsible for what its chatbot told a customer, rejecting the argument that the bot was somebody else's problem.2

1. HVAC Today, National Comfort Institute, reporting an HVAC-Talk thread on ChatGPT.

2. Moffatt v. Air Canada, British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024. Negligent misrepresentation.

What refusing actually looks like

Asked: Which capacities have two outdoor fans?

Not in the knowledge base. The manual doesn't state which capacities have two outdoor fans, and I won't infer it from board diagrams. I've filed this as a gap for product engineering.

Gap #14 filed · 1st ask · routed to product engineering

When the knowledge base doesn't settle a question, Answerlore doesn't produce its best guess in a confident tone. It says “not in the knowledge base,” names the team that owns the answer, and files the question as a gap.

We tested this on a real manufacturer's service manual: asked a question the manual doesn't settle, Answerlore declined to infer the answer from diagrams and logged the gap instead. It also caught the manual contradicting itself about its own version number. Honesty about documents turns out to include honesty about their defects.

The rule underneath

Answerlore never answers from the model's general knowledge. Every factual claim traces to a file in your knowledge base, and every file traces to a source document and page. If the chain is missing, the answer is “not in the knowledge base.” Not because the model can't produce words, but because in support, provenance is the product.

Refusal is a flywheel, not a dead end

Tools that never say “I don't know” can't tell you what your documentation is missing.

A refusal files a gap. Gaps carry a count of how often the question is asked, so your expert answers the most-asked one first, once, in writing. The knowledge base grows in the exact order your customers are asking. Answerlore's gap log is that list, ranked.

“A product that refuses looks worse in a bake-off against one that always answers. We accept that.”

If you want to test us fairly: ask questions your manuals answer, ask ones they don't, and watch which tool tells you the difference.
